# OpenAI — GPT-5.6 Terra

## Executive verdict

MarqtPlaza is a disciplined early-stage concept because it explicitly avoids pretending to be a marketplace, live-data product, or broad local-search competitor before evidence exists. The narrow “This Weekend in Zeeheldenkwartier” wedge is sensible, but the proposed value proposition is still vulnerable to free substitutes that already satisfy much of the discovery need with less effort. The core challenge is not building a bilingual guide; it is proving that manual verification and neighbourhood context create enough incremental value for both repeat users and paying merchants. The EUR 150–300 campaign offer is a reasonable test parameter, but it will be difficult to sell and renew unless campaign reporting can show credible, understandable value without overstating attribution. The business may become a useful editorial/concierge service, but its economics could remain labour-bound unless repeated merchant and user behaviour identifies a narrow workflow worth standardising. Expansion should be earned only after two comparable editions demonstrate repeatable production, demand, merchant conversion, and a plausible contribution margin.  

**Single riskiest assumption:** Independent local businesses will pay repeatedly for a neighbourhood discovery campaign when the service does not own bookings, transactions, a large audience, or provable incremental customer acquisition.

## What not to build yet

1. **Payments, booking, or transaction ownership**  
   These introduce support, refund, reconciliation, fraud, contractual, and potentially regulatory obligations. The pilot should first establish whether discovery and outbound actions are valuable.

2. **Live availability, live opening-hours, or real-time inventory claims**  
   These require dependable integrations or continuous verification. Incorrect “live” information would directly undermine the trust proposition.

3. **An open merchant listing or self-service publishing platform**  
   Open submissions create moderation, duplicates, spam, disputes, inaccurate claims, and ranking pressure. The initial differentiation depends on controlled editorial verification.

4. **User reviews, ratings, comments, or social community features**  
   These create content moderation, defamation, manipulation, and dispute-management burdens. They also shift the product away from curated discovery toward a weaker version of established review platforms.

5. **Algorithmic personalisation or recommendation engines**  
   There will not initially be enough behavioural data to justify them, and personalisation increases privacy complexity. A clear editorial shortlist is more appropriate for the pilot.

6. **Multi-neighbourhood or citywide expansion**  
   Wider coverage multiplies verification and freshness obligations before the team knows the cost and return of one neighbourhood workflow.

7. **A merchant self-serve campaign dashboard**  
   Early campaigns should be delivered manually so founders can learn what merchants actually ask for in reporting, approvals, changes, and renewal conversations.

8. **Aggregate-insights product or personal-data monetisation model**  
   The concept correctly rejects personal-data resale. Aggregate insights should wait until there is sufficient lawful, representative, privacy-protected data and a credible buyer need.

## 30/60/90-day priorities

### Days 1–30: establish a credible pilot operating system

- Define the initial editorial scope precisely: what counts as “This Weekend,” eligible categories, geographical boundary, publishing schedule, and minimum verification standard.
- Build the minimum structured content model for places, event occurrences, sources, claims, checked dates, corrections, commercial labels, and campaigns.
- Publish editorial, corrections, commercial-labelling, privacy, and campaign-term pages in plain Dutch and English where relevant.
- Create a manual verification checklist and a correction ticket workflow.
- Recruit a small set of prospective design partners, but do not represent them as customers unless they have accepted terms or paid.
- Conduct structured interviews with both likely users and merchants.

**Decision gate:** Proceed to a first public edition only if the team can produce a dated shortlist with complete source links, checked dates, correction route, and clearly separated commercial labels without relying on undocumented memory or ad hoc spreadsheets.

### Days 31–60: run two comparable editions and test commercial demand

- Publish two comparable weekend editions using the same inclusion, verification, and measurement rules.
- Track source of traffic, qualified visits, outbound actions, correction volume, verification time, support time, and content-production time.
- Offer the standard 30-day campaign only with written terms, a fixed price within the proposed test range, and explicit non-guarantee language.
- Record every merchant sales conversation: segment, objection, requested proof, accepted deliverables, price response, and reason for acceptance or rejection.
- Test a small number of distribution channels separately rather than mixing all promotion into one launch.
- Produce campaign reports that distinguish observed actions from merchant-reported downstream outcomes.

**Decision gate:** Continue the paid campaign experiment only if at least some merchants accept the actual terms and payment process, and the team can deliver reports without claiming that clicks equal customers or sales. If no merchant accepts, revise the segment, offer, price, or evidence standard before expanding supply.

### Days 61–90: assess repeatability before expansion

- Complete a two-edition scorecard covering user behaviour, correction burden, production time, merchant conversations, invoices, cash collected, direct costs, and valued founder time.
- Compare free verified profiles, paid campaign placements, and distribution channels without allowing campaign status to affect factual listing quality.
- Identify the most repeated operational bottleneck: verification, translation, media permissions, campaign reporting, correction handling, or merchant onboarding.
- Automate only the narrowest repeated workflow that demonstrably saves time or reduces errors.
- Interview campaign buyers and non-buyers after the pilot to determine what evidence they trusted, what they did not understand, and what would cause renewal.
- Decide whether the next step is: repeat in Zeeheldenkwartier, refine the offer, narrow the merchant segment, pause paid campaigns, or expand to one adjacent cluster.

**Decision gate:** Expand geography or category only if the two-edition scorecard shows a documented, repeatable production process; manageable correction and support load; at least one credible recurring demand signal from users or merchants; and a plausible path to contribution after direct costs and valued team time.

## Questions requiring founder evidence

1. What specific user problem is materially better solved by MarqtPlaza than Google Maps, local publisher guides, Instagram, and personal recommendations?
2. Which exact consumer segment is expected to use “This Weekend in Zeeheldenkwartier,” and what evidence supports its bilingual needs?
3. How often do intended users currently seek this type of information, and what would make them return rather than search anew elsewhere?
4. Which merchant category has the strongest reason to buy a EUR 150–300 discovery campaign, and who is the economic buyer?
5. What current marketing alternatives do target merchants use, what do they pay, and what evidence do they require before renewing?
6. What does a fully verified listing or event cost in founder time, including sourcing, checking, translation, updates, corrections, and support?
7. What action can be measured credibly for each merchant type, and which downstream outcomes will remain unobservable?
8. How will the service distinguish editorial inclusion, paid campaign placement, sponsorship, referral arrangements, and partner-funded content in every user-facing surface?
9. What sources are reliable enough for factual claims, what permissions are needed for media or copied details, and how will source disputes be handled?
10. What result from the first two editions would cause the founders to stop, narrow, reprice, or materially change the concept rather than continue expanding?

## Executive verdict

The concept is unusually disciplined for a pre-launch idea: it explicitly refuses to claim traction it doesn't have, draws hard lines against scope creep (no payments, no marketplace, no open listings), and separates paid status from factual/organic inclusion in a way that protects trust. That discipline is a genuine strength. But discipline is not evidence of demand. The plan is essentially "we will build a small trustworthy thing and see if anyone cares," which is a reasonable way to *test* a business, but the document currently reads as if the test design is the strategy rather than a precursor to one. The single riskiest assumption is that a narrow, manually-curated neighbourhood guide generates enough recurring *consumer* attention that merchants are buying real distribution when they pay EUR 150–300 — not just a nicer listing. If consumer repeat-use never materializes, the commercial offer quietly becomes "pay us to write your bilingual description," which is a much weaker and less defensible business than the one implied by the "discovery service" framing. Everything else (pricing, expansion, later revenue lines) is downstream of whether that attention exists and is verifiably attributable to the product rather than to founder outreach effort.

## Complexity legend

- **1 – Trivial for two people:** Hours to ~1 day. Editing docs, writing policy text, manual spreadsheet changes, a single conversation. No new tools.
- **2 – Small effort:** A few days to ~2 weeks. A lightweight process, a script, a simple structured file, one round of testing. Doable alongside other work.
- **3 – Real build:** 2–6 weeks of focused work for one or both founders. Requires an actual feature (tracking mechanism, schema migration, consent flow) and likely takes priority over other tasks during that window.
- **4 – Substantial investment:** 6–12 weeks. Meaningful engineering/legal/compliance work; likely needs external input (legal review, contractor) and forces deprioritization of other roadmap items.
- **5 – Major undertaking:** 3+ months, or requires resources (funding, hires, formal infrastructure) beyond what two founders can absorb alongside running the pilot. Should not be attempted before the core loop is proven.

## What not to build yet

- **Payments/booking infrastructure** — no proven demand yet, and adds compliance and liability burden disproportionate to a 30-day discovery campaign.
- **Open/self-serve listing submission** — undermines the manual-verification trust promise before that trust is established, and invites low-quality or spam entries.
- **Live-availability integrations** (tables, tickets, stock) — technically heavy, dependent on third-party APIs, and outside the stated "dated shortlist" scope.
- **Broad geographic expansion** — dilutes the founder time needed to prove the single-neighbourhood playbook works at all.
- **Open review community** — creates moderation and legal exposure, and directly conflicts with the "editorial, verified" positioning that differentiates from Yelp/Tripadvisor.
- **Native mobile apps** — a lightweight bilingual web guide is sufficient to test the core hypothesis; app store overhead isn't justified pre-traction.
- **Heavy SaaS/automation tooling** — explicitly deferred in the concept already, and correctly so; automating a workflow that isn't yet repeated and understood risks building the wrong thing.
- **Personalization or recommendation algorithms** — requires data volume and behavioral history that a single-neighbourhood pilot won't generate.

# Google — Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview

## Executive verdict
The MarqtPlaza concept demonstrates excellent discipline by actively constraining scope and avoiding the premature scaling traps of typical local-discovery platforms (e.g., live-availability, automated scraping, marketplace dynamics). Framing the initial product as an editorial "concierge" service ensures high quality, builds local trust, and creates a clear wedge against broader, noisier substitutes like Google Maps or Tripadvisor. However, the business model faces a severe headwind: charging small, independent businesses €150–€300 upfront for an awareness campaign with no guaranteed footfall or ROI is notoriously difficult, especially for a new platform with no existing audience. The manual, low-tech verification approach ensures data provenance but fundamentally caps margins and operational scalability. Ultimately, success hinges entirely on the founders' ability to sell the *intangible* value of local brand awareness and expat reach to pragmatic shop owners before runaway operational costs kill the pilot.

**Single riskiest assumption:** Local independent businesses in Zeeheldenkwartier will pay €150–€300 upfront for a 30-day awareness campaign purely on the promise of transparent reporting, without any guarantees of footfall, revenue, or baseline click volume.

*For a two-person founding team:*
*   **1 - Trivial:** Can be executed in hours to a few days using existing no-code tools, basic spreadsheets, or standard copy.
*   **2 - Easy:** Requires up to a week of focused effort; basic web styling, simple integrations (e.g., lightweight forms), or standard legal templating.
*   **3 - Moderate:** Takes 1-2 weeks; custom scripting, lightweight database design, analytics setup, or creating structured sales playbooks.
*   **4 - Hard:** Requires multiple weeks; robust custom architecture, complex attribution modeling without third-party cookies, or overcoming significant behavioral hurdles in sales/marketing.
*   **5 - Severe:** Takes months; deep technical infrastructure, highly sophisticated data pipelines, or fundamentally changing consumer habits at scale.

## What not to build yet
1.  **User Accounts / Logins:** Consumers do not want to create an account just to read a weekend guide. Rely on cookies/local storage if you need to track "repeat use" in the pilot phase.
2.  **Self-Serve Merchant Portals:** Building a secure dashboard for merchants to edit their own profiles is complex. Handle all updates via email/forms manually to learn exactly what merchants want to change.
3.  **Native iOS/Android Apps:** App store friction will kill consumer acquisition. A fast, mobile-optimized responsive web page is vastly superior for an early-stage discovery guide.
4.  **Payment Gateways / Checkout:** Invoicing a handful of test merchants €150 via standard bank transfer (Tikkie/iDEAL links) is entirely sufficient. Do not build Stripe integrations until transaction volume demands it.
5.  **Automated Web Scraping:** This violates the core promise of "manual verification" and introduces messy, unstructured data that will ruin the curated editorial feel.
6.  **Complex Recommendation Algorithms:** The value prop is a "small dated shortlist." Curation should be done by the founders' editorial judgment, not machine learning.
7.  **In-Platform Reviews / Ratings:** Managing a review community requires massive moderation resources to prevent abuse. Stick to factual, verified details and external source links.

## 30/60/90-day priorities

**30-Day Priority: The Audience & Content Test**
*   *Action:* Build the minimal web frontend. Curate "This Weekend in Zeeheldenkwartier" for two consecutive weekends with 10-15 free, high-quality bilingual profiles. Distribute via local channels.
*   *Measurement:* Unique local visitors, outbound clicks, time-on-page.
*   *Decision Gate:* Did we achieve at least 100 organic unique visitors and a 10% outbound click-through rate? If no, the consumer value proposition is failing; do not start sales.

**60-Day Priority: The Merchant Pitch Test**
*   *Action:* Armed with consumer engagement data from month 1, conduct founder-led sales pitches to 15-20 local independent businesses.
*   *Measurement:* Commercial conversations held, objections recorded, signed commercial commitments.
*   *Decision Gate:* Can we secure 3 paid campaigns at €150+ upfront? If merchants refuse, pivot the monetization strategy or the campaign deliverables.

**90-Day Priority: The Operations & Renewal Test**
*   *Action:* Execute the 3 paid campaigns. Track all internal time spent on verification, setup, and reporting. Deliver the end-of-campaign report and ask for a renewal.
*   *Measurement:* Internal hours per campaign, cost of delivery, renewal rate.
*   *Decision Gate:* Did at least 1 merchant renew, and did the campaign take less than 4 hours of internal time to execute? If ops time is too high, the unit economics are structurally unviable.

## Questions requiring founder evidence
1.  Can you provide names/quotes from 3 local business owners who have explicitly stated they would pay €150 upfront for this specific awareness campaign?
2.  If the platform does not guarantee footfall, what specific metric will you put on the end-of-month report to prove the €150 was well spent?

# Cross-engine comparison

## Strongest areas of agreement

1. **The core demand assumption is still unproven.** All three engines treat repeat consumer utility and merchant willingness to renew as the decisive unknowns.
2. **The initial scope should remain narrow.** They support one neighbourhood, one recurring consumer decision, and a deliberately manual pilot before citywide expansion.
3. **The campaign requires stricter economics.** The EUR 150–300 price must be tested against sales, verification, bilingual production, support, correction, reporting, and founder-time costs.
4. **Attribution must be defined before selling outcomes.** The engines consistently distinguish measurable platform actions from visits, customers, and revenue.
5. **Freshness and provenance are core product capabilities.** Checked dates, source evidence, ownership, expiry, and correction history should be designed as structured data rather than editorial notes.
6. **Trust rules need operational enforcement.** Clear policies are insufficient without permissions, audit trails, complaint handling, escalation, and separation of paid and factual content.
7. **Bilingual quality creates real operating cost.** Translation workflow, review ownership, terminology consistency, fallback behaviour, and parity need explicit acceptance criteria.
8. **Heavy SaaS and marketplace features should wait.** Payments, booking, open submissions, review systems, personalization, and broad automation would add risk before the wedge is validated.

## Main differences in emphasis

- **OpenAI** places the strongest emphasis on explicit experiment design, unit economics, falsifiable decision gates, and structured operational controls.
- **Claude** places comparatively more emphasis on strategic contradiction, founder capacity, editorial independence, positioning clarity, and the risk of creating a labour-intensive agency rather than a scalable product.
- **Gemini** places comparatively more emphasis on the technical operating model: freshness state, provenance, bilingual workflow, analytics consent, abuse controls, accessibility, and the path from manual operations to a maintainable system.

## Recommended interpretation

The critiques should not be averaged into one giant backlog. Use their overlap to define the minimum pilot contract:

1. a precise consumer job and neighbourhood boundary;
2. a structured verified-card model with ownership and expiry;
3. a measurable 30-day merchant offer with a cost ledger;
4. privacy-safe event definitions and attribution limits;
5. written correction, sponsorship, and incident procedures;
6. explicit stop, revise, and continue gates based on observed activation, repeat use, contribution, and renewal intent.
